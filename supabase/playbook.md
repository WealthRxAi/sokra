MEDICAL (hospital, physician, lab, imaging, ER):
- Request an itemized bill with CPT/HCPCS codes. Never pay a summary bill.
- Charity care / financial assistance (FAP): a nonprofit hospital must, under IRS 501(r)(4), ESTABLISH and publicise a written FAP. 501(r) does NOT mandate any particular generosity — a compliant hospital can cap free care low. Never tell the person the hospital is required to write off their bill; tell them it is required to have a policy, to publish it, and to give them the application.
- TWO 501(r) CLOCKS, both measured from the FIRST POST-DISCHARGE BILLING STATEMENT, not the date of service:
  * 120 days — the earliest the hospital may begin Extraordinary Collection Actions, and only after notification efforts.
  * 240 days — the MINIMUM APPLICATION PERIOD. The hospital MUST accept and process a FAP application through at least day 240. Most large systems take them well past that.
  Never tell someone their leverage is gone at 120 days. If they are found FAP-eligible later, the hospital must reverse collection actions already taken and refund anything paid above the FAP-determined amount. Say so — people give up because they think they are too late.
- Income vs Federal Poverty Level decides eligibility. 2025 HHS guidelines, 48 contiguous states + DC (CHECK FOR A NEWER YEAR; these are updated each January and may be stale): 1 person $15,650; 2 $21,150; 3 $26,650; 4 $32,150; add ~$5,500 per extra person. ALASKA is roughly 25% higher and HAWAII roughly 15% higher — if the person is in AK or HI, say the threshold is higher and that you are not certain of the exact figure. FAPs differ on which year's guidelines apply and on how they define household and income (gross, annualised from current pay stubs, or last year's return). If the person recently lost income, say their CURRENT annualised income may be what counts, not last year's.
- Many hospitals write off 100% up to 200% FPL with a sliding scale to 300–400%, but this VARIES BY FACILITY. Tell the person to ask for their hospital's own posted FAP and plain-language summary — 501(r) requires it to be public — rather than relying on the general pattern.
- Insured people can qualify. Being uninsured does not hurt the application. Say both.
- Medicaid screening: most FAPs require the person to apply for Medicaid and be DENIED as a condition of eligibility. The denial letter is a required attachment, not an obstacle. Never tell someone to skip or decline the screening to avoid delay — that gets applications closed for failure to cooperate. Tell them to complete it immediately and get the denial in writing. In an ER, ask about Hospital Presumptive Eligibility.
- ALSO SCREEN FOR COVERAGE GOING FORWARD. Someone uninsured with a large medical bill almost always needs insurance more than they need this one bill reduced. Mention the ACA Marketplace (healthcare.gov or their state exchange), that people between roughly 100% and 250% FPL get both premium subsidies and cost-sharing reductions, and that enrollment windows matter. Also flag retroactive options: Medicaid can cover up to 3 months before application for eligible people, and a COBRA election window may still be open.
- Amounts Generally Billed (501(r)(5)): ONCE someone is determined FAP-eligible, the hospital may not charge them more than AGB for emergency or medically necessary care. It is not a discount available on demand before applying. AGB is a calculated percentage that varies by facility — tell the person they can ask the hospital for its AGB percentage, don't guess at it.
- SEPARATE PROVIDER BILLS: ER physicians, surgeons, anesthesiologists, radiologists and pathologists usually bill separately and are often NOT covered by the hospital's FAP. 501(r) requires the FAP to list which providers are and are not covered. Tell the person to ask for that list and to expect to apply separately with each group. This is the single most common reason people think they are finished when they are not.
- No Surprises Act: for INSURED people, out-of-network emergency care and out-of-network providers at in-network facilities can only bill in-network cost sharing, so balance bills in those cases are often illegal. For UNINSURED/self-pay people the NSA works differently — the relevant tools are the Good Faith Estimate for scheduled care and Patient-Provider Dispute Resolution when the final bill exceeds the GFE by $400 or more. Do not cite insured-patient protections to a self-pay person.
- Insurance denial or a bill the plan should have covered → this is a COVERAGE APPEAL, not a charity case. Compare the Explanation of Benefits against the bill, appeal in writing within the plan's window (usually 180 days), and escalate to external review if the internal appeal fails. Most reversed denials are reversed for coding or documentation reasons. For Medicare, name the Medicare appeal levels. If the person has insurance and thinks the bill should have been covered, this lever comes FIRST and charity care is usually irrelevant.
- Prompt-pay / cash discount: 20–40% is common. Price transparency rules require hospitals to publish cash prices; if a charge is far above the hospital's own cash price or ~150–200% of the Medicare rate, use that as the anchor.
- Interest-free payment plans from the hospital are standard — never accept interest on medical debt.
- THIRD-PARTY PATIENT FINANCING IS A TRAP AND YOU MUST WARN ABOUT IT whenever a payment plan is discussed. When someone asks for a payment plan, hospitals frequently route them to an outside lender or medical credit card (AccessOne, ClearBalance, CareCredit and similar). The hospital is then paid in full and the person owes a consumer lender instead. The moment that happens: 501(r) protections are gone because there is no hospital balance left to apply against; deferred-interest terms can bite at 20%+ later; and it appears on their credit report as revolving debt when a hospital balance would not. The exact words to use: "Is this a payment plan with the hospital itself, or through an outside company? I only want one directly with the hospital." Say this even when the offer is advertised as 0%.
- Credit reporting of medical debt — these are VOLUNTARY nationwide credit bureau practices, not statute, and the law here has moved more than once recently, so present them as "currently" and tell the person to verify: medical collections under $500 generally not reported; paid medical collections generally removed; roughly a 1-year grace before unpaid medical debt is reported. A large balance IS reportable. If a collector is involved, send a debt validation letter within 30 days of first contact (FDCPA).

CREDIT CARD:
- Hardship program: call and ask for it by name — temporary APR cut (often to 0–10%), waived fees, fixed payments. Available at most major issuers; not advertised.
- Fee and APR reduction call: one call waives a late fee ~70% of the time for a cardholder in good standing; ask for a lower APR citing competing offers.
- Dispute any charge you don't recognize (60-day window from statement under FCBA).
- Debt management plan via an NFCC nonprofit agency: creditors cut rates to ~6–10%, one payment.
- Settlement: only realistic 90+ days delinquent or charged off; 30–50% lump sum is typical. Settled debt is reported; forgiven amount >$600 may be taxable (1099-C).
- Balance transfer only if a 0% promo and the person can pay within the window.

COLLECTIONS (any debt sold or placed with a collector):
- Debt validation letter within 30 days of RECEIPT of the first written notice (FDCPA §1692g). Collector must stop collecting until it verifies. Many debt buyers cannot produce the chain of title. Give an actual mail-by DATE, not "before the window closes." Also say what is true when the window passes: missing 30 days does NOT forfeit the right to dispute — it only loses the automatic cease-collection — and under §1692g(c) failure to dispute is NOT an admission that the debt is owed. People panic about this deadline and panic makes them phone the collector, which is the one thing they must not do.
- TWO SEPARATE CLOCKS. Never conflate them, and state both whenever a debt is old:
  * STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS (state law, commonly 3–6 years) governs only whether they can SUE. It usually runs from the date of default/breach, which may differ from the last payment date. Where a state's rule depends on whether a revolving account counts as a written contract or an open account, say the answer is contested rather than asserting a single figure.
  * CREDIT REPORTING PERIOD (FCRA §605(a)(4)) is 7 years plus 180 days from the date of first delinquency on the ORIGINAL account. A debt can be legally unenforceable and still sit on a credit report for years. If someone cares about their credit, winning the lawsuit question changes nothing for them.
- If time-barred: making a payment OR a written acknowledgment can RESTART the clock in many states. Never pay or promise to pay on an old debt before checking. A collector's "helpful" small monthly plan is the most common way this trap is sprung — call it out explicitly.
- TO AFFECT THE CREDIT REPORT you need a DIFFERENT letter to a DIFFERENT party: an FCRA §611 dispute to Equifax, Experian and TransUnion directly, which triggers a 30-day reinvestigation. If the collector cannot verify to the bureau, the tradeline comes off. The FDCPA validation letter goes to the collector; the FCRA dispute goes to the bureaus. Include BOTH when the person's goal is their credit.
- Free federal leverage people don't know about: a CFPB complaint at consumerfinance.gov/complaint costs nothing and carries a response obligation — often more pressure than a letter, especially against a large debt buyer. Also the state attorney general. Mention these before recommending anything paid.
- Settlement, only if the debt is actually enforceable and the person wants it resolved: 30–50% is typical, agreed IN WRITING before any money moves. "Pay for delete" is worth asking for but the bureaus' furnisher agreements discourage it, so most collectors refuse in writing — do not promise it. A settled account is reported as "settled for less than full balance," which a mortgage underwriter will read. Forgiven amounts over $600 may generate a 1099-C.
- If the person is applying for a mortgage, say so plainly: FHA guidance generally requires non-medical collections with an aggregate balance over $2,000 to be paid, placed on a documented plan, or counted in DTI at 5% of the balance, and lender overlays vary. A collection that is unenforceable can still block a loan.
- Everything in writing. Never give a collector bank account access.

UTILITY (electric, gas, water):
- LIHEAP (energy assistance) and state/utility hardship funds — most people eligible never apply.
- Budget billing to flatten payments; medical-necessity disconnect protections; winter/summer moratoria in many states.
- Payment arrangement before disconnect; ask for late fees waived once.
- Dispute unusual spikes — request a meter read / test.

TELECOM / INTERNET / PHONE:
- Call retention ("I'm thinking of cancelling") — repricing to current promo is routine.
- Remove line items: equipment fees, protection plans, premium channels, "administrative" fees.
- Lifeline / ACP-successor programs for low-income households.

AUTO / PERSONAL LOAN:
- Deferment or extension for hardship (1–3 months, interest usually accrues).
- Loan modification or refinance at a credit union (often 2–5 points lower).
- Compare voluntary surrender vs repossession costs honestly if underwater.

STUDENT LOAN (federal):
- Income-driven repayment — payment can be $0 at low income. Deferment/forbearance for hardship. PSLF if public-sector employer. Never pay a company to "help" with federal loans.

RENT / HOUSING:
- Emergency rental assistance (county/city), written payment plan, know notice periods before any eviction can proceed.

TAX (IRS / state):
- First-time penalty abatement (one phone call, often granted). Installment agreement online if under $50k. Currently Not Collectible status if hardship. Offer in Compromise if truly unable to pay.

INSURANCE PREMIUMS / OTHER:
- Shop, re-rate, raise deductible, bundle; ask for hardship programs; dispute billing math.

ORDER OF OPERATIONS — levers are not independent and doing the easy one first can destroy the valuable one. This is the most common way people lose money, so enforce it:
- Set "blocked_by" on any lever that must WAIT for another, naming the lever it waits on, and say in one plain sentence what doing it early would cost.
- Medical: never negotiate a discount, offer a lump sum, or commit to a payment plan BEFORE a financial-assistance decision. Offering money signals ability to pay and can be used against the application; and someone later found eligible may be owed a refund they will have to fight for. A person at or under ~200% FPL should be told clearly NOT to pay anything until the FAP decision comes back.
- Collections: never pay, promise to pay, or acknowledge a possibly time-barred debt before the statute question is settled.
- Credit card: the hardship/APR ask is a phone call and comes first; written disputes run alongside it.
- The one-line defensive sentence every medical caller should have: "I'm not agreeing to a payment plan until my financial assistance application is decided."
